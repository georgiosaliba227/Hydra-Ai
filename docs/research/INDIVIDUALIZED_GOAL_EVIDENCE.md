# Evidence for an individualized daily beverage goal

**Research decision · November 4, 2026.** Hydra needs an estimate in millilitres of beverages per person per day. The studies reviewed here support research on candidate predictors. They leave the accuracy and safety of a daily beverage goal based on age, height, weight and supplements unresolved. Automatic goal setting requires further evidence and separate product approval.

## What a goal would measure

| Quantity | Meaning for this decision |
| --- | --- |
| Total body water (L) | The amount of water present in the body at one time. A change in this amount gives no recurring daily intake by itself. |
| Water turnover (L/day) | Water entering or leaving the body over time. Sources include food, beverages and water produced by metabolism. |
| Total dietary or preformed water (L/day) | Water consumed in foods and drinks. Separating drinks requires information about how much water came from food. |
| Beverage volume (mL/day) | The volume of all drinks consumed. Beverage composition determines their water content. Hydra can log confirmed drink volumes. |
| Individual beverage goal (mL/day) | A proposed intake intended to achieve a defined outcome for that person. Predicting what someone habitually drinks answers a different question from establishing a suitable goal. |

## What the candidate models predict

**Dolci et al. (2022)** studied 557 adults aged 19–51 with BMI 18–30 kg/m² across 1,575 visits. They split participants 75/25 and used 23 predictors including age, sex, height, weight, food and fluid intake. Internal testing yielded a urine-osmolality mean absolute error of 124.99 mOsm/kg and 85.5% threshold-classification accuracy. Their optimizer varies plain water to target 500 mOsm/kg. The authors call for prospective clinical validation. Code and data may be released on reasonable request with Danone legal and compliance agreement. We could not verify publicly deployable model weights. [Scientific Reports paper and availability statements](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9669042/)

**Yamada et al. (2022)** measured water turnover by isotope dilution across 5,604 people in 23 countries. Their published equation uses body weight, age, sex, activity and environmental factors and explains 47.1% of turnover variation. That statistic supplies neither an individual error in mL/day of drinks nor a conversion to drink volume. A person's food water and other water inputs would need to be accounted for, and a suitable intake would still need independent validation. [Science accepted manuscript, equation 1 and discussion](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10158948/1/Science_accepted.pdf)

**Watanabe et al. (2024)** studied 141 adults in Kyoto–Kameoka, Japan, in May–June, mean age 72.6 years. They published equations for turnover and preformed water using questionnaire inputs including usual beverage intake. A random split of the same cohort allocated 71 people to development and 70 to validation. Spearman correlations with isotope-based estimates were 0.527 for turnover and 0.477 for preformed water. These measure ranking within one older population and season. They give no individual accuracy estimate for a beverage goal in the wider adult population. [Public Health Nutrition paper, methods and table 4](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980024001587)

The two isotope studies publish their equations. Reproducing their calculations from coefficients is feasible. This review did not establish access to individual-level records for independent reanalysis.

## Supplements and wearable measurements

| Input | Evidence from people | Consequence for the goal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Creatine | A 28-day placebo-controlled study of 32 resistance-trained adults found increased total body water and body mass. A randomized crossover study in 12 active men found similar hydration and heat tolerance after a week of supplementation during exercise in heat. | A change in stored body water does not establish how much extra fluid to drink every day. Omit a dose-based increment. [Body-water trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC155510/) · [Exercise trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1421496/) |
| Electrolytes | In DASH-Sodium, 412 adults with elevated blood pressure received three dietary sodium levels over four weeks each. Higher sodium increased thirst on both diets. Twenty-four-hour urine volume increased only on the control diet. Sports guidance individualizes replacement using sweat losses under comparable exercise conditions. | A supplement serving supplies no validated daily-volume coefficient. Count the confirmed volume of a mixed drink once and record known electrolyte content separately. [DASH-Sodium trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7278848/) · [NATA position statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/) |
| Caffeine | In a three-day crossover comparison involving 50 habitual male coffee drinkers, moderate coffee and equal-volume water gave similar hydration measurements. In a separate crossover study of ten adults, a high single caffeine dose increased urine output over three hours at rest. | Record the coffee volume actually consumed. Neither acute urine output nor caffeine dose establishes a daily replacement volume. [Habitual coffee study](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0084154) · [Acute dose study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28868290/) |
| Heart rate and HRV | In 20 adults exercising in heat, a model used heart-rate responses to scripted changes of posture to classify a 2% body-mass loss. It measured discrimination under that protocol. | The study provides no daily-volume calibration for passive watch heart rate or HRV. Exclude both from goal calculation and avoid inferring dehydration from either. [Wearable study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34210068/) |

No reviewed source validates a universal supplement allowance or a fixed mL/kg/day rule for this product endpoint. Omission of a supplement coefficient means its effect on the target remains unquantified.

## Evidence needed before activation

1. **Define the claim.** Specify the intended adult population, exclusions, beverage types, time horizon and outcome a goal should support. Define with qualified clinical reviewers what would count as meaningful benefit and unacceptable over- or under-recommendation. Plan how the app will abstain with missing inputs, outside the studied population, or for people with prescribed fluid restrictions.
2. **Establish data access and permitted use.** Before any author contact, obtain authorization. Request the fitted model, preprocessing, feature dictionary and units, missing-data rules, participant-level split or equivalent reproduction material, and study inclusion criteria. Ask separately about research use, redistribution and on-device use. Record version, provenance and permissions. An article's open-access license alone does not establish rights to distribute its fitted model.
3. **Test predictions in independent people.** Freeze the model, intended inputs and analysis plan before testing. Use people and sites outside model development. Obtain appropriately consented reference measurements over repeated days, with beverage volume, food water and relevant physiological outcomes recorded separately. Compare against a simple prespecified baseline. For beverage-volume predictions report mean absolute error and signed bias in mL/day, individual error distributions, interval coverage and subgroup performance. Report biomarker errors separately in their native units. Agreement with observed intake alone supports an intake estimate.
4. **Test the proposed goal prospectively.** Under an independently reviewed protocol, compare outcomes when people follow the proposed goal versus an appropriate comparator. Assess adherence, benefit, harms and uncertainty in the intended population against prespecified criteria. Evaluate each proposed supplement adjustment for additional benefit and safety before using it. A participant holdout within the original dataset cannot answer this intervention question.
5. **Review the activation decision.** Require documented evidence, rights for intended use, clinical and product review, and explicit approval. Software tests can establish correct units, calculation, input validation and abstention. Physiological validity requires the independent human evidence above.
